\section{Rotational Motion under Constant Angular Acceleration}
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Rotational motion:} An object rotates \textbf{around an axis}.
	\begin{itemize}
		\item The axis of rotation can change.
		\item For example, the Earth rotates on its own axis.
	\end{itemize}

	\item \textbf{Circular motion:} a type of a two dimensional motion,
		along \textbf{the circumference of a circle}.
	\begin{itemize}
		\item The distance between the centre of mass and the axis of rotation remains \textbf{fixed}.
		\item For example, artificial satellites orbit the Earth at a fixed altitude.
	\end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

If angular velocity changes at a constant rate:
angular acceleration is constant and
the rotational motion is under \textbf{constant angular acceleration}

\begin{align}
	\alpha &= \frac{\omega - \omega_0}{\tau} \nonumber \\
	\omega &= \omega_0 + \alpha\tau \\
	\text{Average angular speed: } \bar{\omega} &=  \frac{\omega + \omega_0}{2} \\
	\theta &= \bar{\omega}\tau \nonumber \\
	\theta &= \omega_0\tau + \frac12 \alpha\tau^2 \\
	\omega^2 &= \omega_0\tau + 2 \alpha \theta
\end{align}

Units:
\begin{align*}
	\text{angular displacement: } & \text{radian } (rad) = 2\pi \\
	\text{angular velocity: } & \text{radian per second } (rad/s) \\
	\text{angular acceleration: } & \text{radian per second squared } (rad/s^2)
\end{align*}

\section{Relation Between Angular \& Linear Quantities}
All particles of the body move in a circle perpendicular to the axis

The direction of linear velocity is tangent to the path called \textbf{tangential velocity}

When linear velocity change, the object have \textbf{tangential acceleration}

$ a_r = r\alpha $

\textbf{The right-hand rule} \\
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{The fingers}: direction of rotation
	\item \textbf{The thumb}: direction of angular velocity vector
\end{itemize}

The angular velocity is:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{increasing:} angular acceleration vector points in the \textbf{same} direction
	\item \textbf{decreasing:} angular acceleration vector points in the \textbf{opposite} direction
\end{itemize}

\section{Centripetal Acceleration}
\begin{itemize}
	\item Constant speed $v$: \textbf{uniform circular motion}
	\item The magnitude of the velocity remains constant but the direction of velocity continuously changes
	\item Continuously accelerated even when speed is constant $ (v_1 = v_2 = v) $
	\item $\Delta t$ approaches zero: \textbf{instantaneous acceleration}
	\item $\Delta t$ is very small: $\Delta s$ \& $\Delta \theta$ are also very small
		and $\vec{v_2} \parallel \vec{v_1}$ 
	\item $\Delta\vec{v}$ perpendicular to both of them
		and points toward the center of the circle
	\item Acceleration $\vec{a}$ is in the same direction as $\Delta\vec{v}$,
		and is \textbf{centripetal acceleration}
	\item The centripetal (radial) acceleration can be determined as follow:

\end{itemize}

Therefore, 
\begin{align}
	a_c &= \frac{v^2}{r} \\
	v &= r\omega \\
	a_c &= r\omega^2
\end{align}

For \textbf{uniform circular motion},
centripetal acceleration vector points towards the center
while the linear velocity vector is tangential

Centripetal acceleration \& linear velocity are perpendicular to each other

For \textbf{non-uniform circular motion},
angular speed is changing and the object experiences the angular acceleration

There is tangential acceleration in addition to centripetal acceleration

\begin{align}
	\vec{a} &= \vec{a_c} + \vec{a_r}
\end{align}

Unlike tangential acceleration,
centripetal acceleration is present in both uniform \& non-uniform circular motion.

\begin{align}
	a &= \sqrt{a_c^2 + a_r^2} \\
	tan \phi &= \frac{a_r}{a_c}
\end{align}


\section{Forces in Circular Motion}
2 basic forces:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Centripetal force:} towards the centre of the circular path
	\item \textbf{Centrifugal force:} away from the centre
\end{itemize}

Newton's third law: the ball exerts a reaction force on the hand

When the car is moving with constant velocity along a straight road:
there is no net force on the person

When the car moves around a curve:
the person feels a \textbf{outward force}---centrifugal force---exerted on him

Centrifugal force is
caused by the effect of inertia and is not a real force
an apparent or fictitious force: equal \& opposite to the centripetal force

\subsection{Forces causing Centripetal Acceleration}
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Newton's second law:} an object in circular motion requires
		a net force directed towards the centre of the circle called \textbf{centripetal force}
	\item \textbf{Newton's first law:} In absence of this force, object would not move along a circle but in a straight line
	\item \textbf{Law of Inertia:} $F=0$, $a=0$, $v=\text{constant velocity}$

	\item Using Newton's second law,
		\begin{equation}
			F_\text{net} = ma
		\end{equation}
	\item The magnitude of centripetal force is
	\begin{align}
		F_c &= ma_c = \frac{mv^2}{r} \\
		\shortintertext{Substituting}
		v &= r\omega \\
		F_c &= mr\omega^2
	\end{align}

	\item Centripetal forces are the tension force of string, rope, or cables pulling an object
		along a curved path toward the center of the circle
	\item For example, to swing a ball on the end of a string in a circle,
		the string is pulled and exerts the force (tension) on the ball.
	\item The tension in the string provides the required centripetal force
		for the ball to keep moving in circular path

	\item Centripetal forces are the gravitational force of satellites orbiting things.
		\begin{itemize}
			\item Gravitational force of the earth on the moon orbiting it
			\item Gravitational force of the sun on the planets orbiting it
			\item Electric force acting on an electron orbiting the nucleus
		\end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
Different Forms of Centripetal Force
Different forces can provide centripetal force
depending on the motion of bodies under various conditions
\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{Tension force} ($T$)
	\item \textbf{Gravitational force} ($F_G$)
	\item \textbf{Electric force} ($F_e$)
	\item \textbf{Frictional force} ($F_f$)
	\item \textbf{Normal force} ($F_N$)
	\item \textbf{Upward thrust} (Lifting Force) on a plane force ($F_L$)
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection*{Frictional Force ($F_f$)}
\begin{itemize}
	\item Frictional force exist between two surfaces.
	\item For example, when a car is turning frictional force
		acts normally to the direction of motion of the car---towards
		the centre of the turn---behaving like a centripetal force.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{Normal Force ($F_N$)}
\begin{itemize}
	\item For example, when a car moves in a circle path
		which  is inclined to the horizontal (banked),
		the reaction force of the road acts \textbf{normally} to the car.
	\item 2 components: horizontal component acts towards the centre of the circle.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{Upward Thrust (Lifting Force) on a Plane ($F_L$)}
\begin{itemize}
	\item Upward thrust acts normally to an aeroplane body.
	\item It causes the aeroplane to turn in a curve.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{Banking of Roads}
On a flat road (unbanked road) the force is supplied
by friction between the tyres and the road.

\begin{equation}
	F_f = \frac{mv^2}{r}
\end{equation}

Banking of roads is raising the outer edge of the curve
higher than the inner edge.

Angle that the inclined track makes with horizontal
is called \textbf{angle of banking}.
The normal force $F_N$ exerted is perpendicular to the road.

Horizontal component of the normal force, $F_Nsin\theta$,
\begin{equation}
	F_N\sin\theta = \frac{mv^2}{r}
\end{equation}
Since there is no vertical motion, the net force on car in y direction is zero.
\begin{align}
	F_N\sin\theta + (-mg) &= 0 \\
	F_N\cos\theta &= mg \\
	\intertext{Hence,}
	\frac{F_N\sin\theta}{F_N\cos\theta} &=
	\frac{mv^2}{r} \times \frac{1}{mg} \\
	\tan\theta &= \frac{v^2}{rg}
\end{align}
